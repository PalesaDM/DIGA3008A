Week 11 - Blog Post: Critical Reflection

5 May 2025

Digital Inequalities in the Age of Artificial Intelligence and Big Data

Christopher Lutz’s 2019 reading (Digital Inequalities in the Age of Artificial Intelligence and Big Data) gives us points of research on how deriving technologies such as AI and big data restructure the platform and foundation of digital inequality. He argues that digital inequalities are not focused on the aspect of connectivity only but are intersected with larger sociocultural and socio economically constructions and competence dynamics.

Lutz critically focuses on algorithms and the biasses around how they affect marginalised communities. He further argues that the categorisation of artificial intelligence systems often lack aspects of transparency and accountability which leads to choices made that result in augmented current social disparities. An example of this is looking at a corporate space and realising that AI recruitment tools approve certain groups of people more than the other as they trace important key words in their CV’s and favour these groups because of that. This is sometimes inaccurate and may be deemed as unfair and unethical as well.

At this point, Lutz points out the rise of the ‘data divide’, where when we focus mainly on data collection as well as data analysis, it becomes apparent that the benefits of the two are evidently distributed unevenly. While governments and major corporations heavily depend on a variety of data sets for decision making, individuals often times do not have access to their own data nor the resources to interpret it. This raises concerns on the idea of privacy and consent in today’s digital age. The idea of digital self-determination becomes relevant, emphasising the person’s right to complete control of the digital footprint and all associated to the data of their presence.

In conclusion, Christopher Lutz’s analysis proves that tackling inequalities in the digital world requires a sophisticated approach in which there will be an improvement in digital literacy, fair data practices and an involvement of ethical practices in the digital space and the development of AI. With that being said, this should follow an update of policies laying an understanding of how digital tools cause a division with social structures and integrating the government and stakeholders in the design of digital systems.

Reference

Lutz, C. (2019). Digital inequalities in the age of artificial intelligence and big data. Human Behavior and Emerging Technologies, 1(2), 141–148. https://doi.org/10.1002/hbe2.140
