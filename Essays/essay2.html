Essay 2: The Internet, Geopolitics, and digital coloniality

By Palesa M · 13 June, 2025

Introduction

Digital coloniality refers to the prolongation of colonial power and control in digital aspects of infrastructures, data and algorithms by organizations that are part of the Global North being developed countries such as the United Sates and China

Looking at this from a geopolitical perspective, economic dependencies and governance can be shaped through sovereignty being supreme power. With the focus being on the major powers which are China and the United States, this essay will crucially discuss how their organizations develop and maintain AI and data-centre infrastructure across Africa while also drawing from Graham’s concept of Internet geographies (2014) and also Ballatore et al.’s discourse on digital hegemonies (2017).

The aim is for the essay to expose how there is an ongoing issue of digital investment and geopolitical struggle occurring in African regions accompanied by who is given authority to regulate data and dominion inequalities in the current digital age.

How power & control work in digital spaces

Graham (2014) describes the internet to be a geographical landscape that is not balanced and is moulded by the positioning of infrastructure, regulation and corporate power. Some places have fully adjusted to this and take advantage of the digital flow, while others don’t.

He states that the internet is not as limitless and transparent as we imagine it to be. Instead of it promoting transparency it contradicts this by exposing geographical inequalities. The three articles being Ballatore, Graham, and Sen (2017) further gather information on the activity of search engines and what is made public to view online.

Their research proves that worldwide tech companies hierarchize trends and results in powerful and influential countries, leaving content from locals out and domineering specific narratives. These studies combined, show an unsettling reality of digital and geopolitical hierarchies that we unfortunately must experience.

Mini Case Studies: AI Infrastructures in Africa

1.Microsoft and G2A in Kenya Part of the $1billion plan was to bring AI services to Africa therefore a project commenced in Kenya 2024 where Microsoft partnered with the UAE based AI firm G2A. The goal of the project was to invest in a geothermal powered data centre in Kenya meaning the energy used to create electricity in the data centre is from the Earth's core and is captured and heated to harness. They also aimed for inclusivity by promising models that could understand local languages in Kenya including the first language being Swahili. While this seems like a power move, it was just a political strategy from the US to compete with China’s rapidly growing tech (Financial Times, 2024).

2.World Bank Funding for Raxio With there being a clear infrastructure gap in Africa, the World Bank’s IFC arm deposited an amount of $100 million in Raxio which is Africa’s leading carrier-neutral data centre operator with the hopes of closing the gap by expanding these data centres across East and Central Africa (Reuters, 2025). However, majority of this infrastructure is owned and managed by foreigners and not Africans themselves which raises major worries about empowering and uplifting the locals while also safe-guarding long-term control amongst Africans.

3.China’s Smart Cities and Surveillance Systems Concurrently, China is doing exceptionally well at its development in the tech space and is making rapid progress. China is currently working on a project of exporting smart-city technology in Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Shenzen and Hangzhou through big companies such as Huawei. These advanced technologies include surveillance cameras, facial recognition, and traffic management tools. These were advertised for the Chinese niche but now many African governments currently receive their services from Chinese infrastructure for surveillance and data analytics (CSIS, 2024).

Power, Dependence, and Digital Hegemony

All three case studies above show how geopolitical leverage is present even in African countries that are trying to improve their digital infrastructure. People should not only focus into what tech does but should also investigate who controls it as this has a direct impact on the digital future. Kenya’s data centre provides local cloud services but companies such as Microsoft and G42 are in the backend with the responsibility of decision-making and design, stripping Kenya from total control. This will have an impact on Kenya’s potential accumulation of foreign engagement. China’s smart city is also a great example of power as their systems have full control over how other countries are run and monitored which could have an influence on political styles and values.

Ballatore assists us in comprehending that there a much deeper side of algorithmic power. AI models can be trained to perform certain tasks but that does not mean that they will adjust properly into the environment where these tasks are required to be performed and reflect local needs or values. A model may be able to speak Swahili but if its original place of training is Abu Dhabi, it may still have societal beliefs of the environment embedded in it. The same applies to China’s surveillance systems that are embedded with algorithms that suit China and if brought to another country, the possibility of them not fitting with African values and priorities is high.

There is cycle repeating itself of old colonial models but instead of gold or land being the main issue, data is falling victim of this. We are witnessing control through online platforms, algorithms and infrastructure in countries. This pattern or cycle is one put in place for African counties, governments and locals to have a dependent mindset and to remain disempowered while digital colonialism strives and gives the Global North and China an upper hand to benefit and take advantage of the digital developments of Africa. Timcke (2024) and Barrett et al. (2025) back this up by stating that there may be digital progress occurring, but colonialism and power struggles are only taking us back to the old colonial models.

The next step from here?

Moving forward, I think African countries should prioritise the implementation of strategies to maintain control over their resources. They should also practice independency as Ballatore et al. (2017) states that there should be diversity and avoid relying on just one or two providers for digital tools but instead have a pool of options to choose from, while also building great relationships with other companies. Failure to implemented this will lead to a client digital state in the Africa with systems that do not align with our values and those that we have zero control over. A good way of doing this is implementing laws and regulations for data protection, working towards building home grown companies to avoid digital coloniality and being part of contracts with global firms that do not disadvantage them. Data centres should consider reducing internet costs, try to not assert dormancy and advocate more for local businesses. This can be done successfully with the support of a firm governance that will be active to avoid countries having unequal relationships with one another. With this executed in the rightful manner, dependency will begin even for the World Bank’s Raxio initiative if they do not involve local ownership and deliberation.

Conclusion

We have established that as AI and cloud computing become fundamental aspects that influence Africa’s global economy, the digital future of our Continent is slowly being reshaped into something it is not. Digital power struggles uprise when countries such as the U.S and China decide to invest in the developments which are often filtered as opportunities for development but also have a negative side to them of power and control. With these issues of digital colonialism being exposed, it is important for African nations to embark on a journey of digital independence through infrastructure, architecture and governance. This is the only plan that could work to assert independency and empowerment in the Continent.
